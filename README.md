Your cover sells the click. Your interior sells the next book. Readers
rarely compliment good typesetting — they just keep reading, which is the
whole point. Bad typesetting, on the other hand, announces itself on
every page: rivers of white space running down paragraphs, chapter
openings crammed against the top margin, body text set too tight to
read comfortably for an hour.

For independent authors publishing on KDP, the interior has traditionally
been the hardest part to get right. Word fights you: styles drift,
section breaks misbehave, and what looks fine on screen reflows
differently in print. Professional typesetters solve this with tools
built for the job. One of those tools is LaTeX, the document system
that has typeset most of the scientific and technical literature for
decades — and it is increasingly accessible to authors who have never
written a line of code, because modern templates pair the LaTeX files
with instructions for working alongside an AI coding assistant.

This tutorial walks through that workflow end to end: choosing a design,

checking the result before it ever reaches KDP.

## What to look for before you commit

Never buy a template you have not inspected. A reputable template shop
shows you complete sample PDFs — not just a hero image of a cover, but
the actual pages your readers will hold. When you open a sample, check
these four things:

1. **The contents page.** It should breathe: generous white space,
   clear hierarchy, page numbers that align. If the contents page looks
   cramped, the chapters will too.
2. **A chapter opening.** Look for a clear drop cap or chapter number,
   an epigraph if the design uses one, and — critically — space above
   the first line. A chapter that starts hard against the top margin
   feels cheap no matter how good the typeface is.
3. **A dense body-text spread.** Read two full pages. Is the line
   length comfortable (roughly 60–75 characters)? Is the leading
   generous enough that your eye finds the next line easily? Do
   paragraphs break cleanly, without widows and orphans stranded at
   page edges?
4. **The front and back matter.** Title page, copyright page, and any
   section dividers should feel like the same book as the chapters —
   same type family, same rules, same spacing logic. Coordinated front
   matter, chapters, and cover are what separate a designed book from
   a formatted manuscript.

## The workflow, step by step

### Step 1: Choose a design you love

Browse the collection and pick the visual style that fits your book, not
the one that looks most impressive in isolation. A historical narrative
wants a different texture than a business book or a poetry collection.
Download the sample PDF for your shortlist and read it like a reader,
not like a shopper. The design you stop noticing after three pages is
the right one — good typography disappears.

### Step 2: Make it your own

Each package includes editable LaTeX files plus instructions for working
with an AI coding assistant. In practice, the loop looks like this:

- **Add your manuscript.** Your text goes into the chapter files,
  marked up with simple commands for headings, emphasis, and breaks.
  If your manuscript is in Word or Google Docs, export to plain text
  first and re-apply structure deliberately — this is a feature, not a
  chore, because it strips out years of accumulated formatting cruft.
- **Set your titles and author details.** Title page, running heads,
  and copyright page are separate small files; changing them is a
  matter of editing a few lines.
- **Adjust typography and page dimensions.** Font sizes, margins, and
  trim size are parameters, not mouse-dragging. This is where the AI
  assistant earns its keep: describe the change you want in plain
  language ("make the chapter titles smaller and add more space above
  them"), let it edit the files, recompile, and look at the result.
  Iterate in small steps and keep a copy of the last version that
  looked right.

You will need a local LaTeX setup or a compatible online editor —
either works; the online route has nothing to install and is the
faster start if you have never used LaTeX.

### Step 3: Bring the book to life

Compile the interior and cover PDFs, then check the finished pages
before you upload anything:

- **Read the PDF on a tablet at 100%.** Skim every chapter opening and
  read two spreads in full. You are checking for the same four things
  as in the sample inspection, now with your text in place.
- **Verify against KDP's current requirements.** Trim size, margins,
  bleed, and PDF settings change from time to time — always check the
  current KDP specifications rather than trusting any tutorial,
  including this one. Your template's page dimensions must match the
  trim size you select in KDP.
- **Check the cover separately.** The cover PDF has its own geometry
  (spine width depends on page count). Compile it last, after the
  interior page count is final.

Only when the PDFs look right do you upload them to your print service.

## Honest limits

A template is a starting point, not a verdict. Three things it will not
do for you:

- **It will not fix a manuscript that needs editing.** Typesetting
  polishes presentation; it cannot repair structure, pacing, or prose.
  Finish your edits first.
- **It will not replace typographic judgment.** The AI assistant can
  execute "more space above chapter titles," but deciding how much
  space is right is your call, made by looking at pages. Budget an
  hour of looking.
- **LaTeX has a learning curve.** The first compile feels alien if you
  have only ever used Word. The second one feels normal. The template's
  included instructions exist precisely to flatten that curve — follow
  them literally the first time, improvise the second.
